          - paragraph [ref=e298]: The final set compares fast pulling with fast full-stroke freestyle. Notice how the addition of your kick changes your rhythm and speed. Your pull should remain effective rather than becoming shorter or more frantic.
          - paragraph [ref=e299]: Speed work depends on adequate recovery. Rest long enough to produce meaningful speed on the next repetition. If every round becomes slower, you’re no longer training the same quality.
          - paragraph [ref=e300]: Fast doesn’t mean uncontrolled. Maintain a stable head position, direct pressure backward during your pull, and keep your kick connected to the rhythm of your stroke. If one component begins to break down, reduce your effort slightly.
          - paragraph [ref=e301]: Record your times for selected 25s if possible. Look for repeatable speed rather than one exceptional effort. The objective is to swim technically sound and fast consistently.
          - 'heading "Workout 5: Hold Your Freestyle Technique as You Tire" [level=2] [ref=e302]'
          - paragraph [ref=e303]: "This 3,700-yard endurance workout challenges you to maintain your pull, kick, and breathing mechanics during progressively demanding freestyle. Each major set prioritizes one component of the stroke: kicking during the first set, breathing during the distance progression, and pulling during the final group of 100s."
          - paragraph [ref=e304]: Most swimming should remain between 5 and 7 on a scale of 1 to 10. The 300 and 200 in the middle of the workout should be faster than the longer repeat before them, but they shouldn’t become all-out efforts. Your objective is to preserve your chosen technical focus while increasing your pace.
          - paragraph [ref=e305]: You’ll need a pull buoy and kickboard. Because this is a long workout, reduce the number of rounds or repetitions if the distance exceeds your current training level. A shorter session completed with sound technique is more useful than finishing 3,700 yards after your stroke has deteriorated substantially.

          - heading "How to Maintain Good Freestyle Technique While Tired" [level=3] [ref=e344]
          - paragraph [ref=e345]: Endurance swimming isn’t only about completing more distance. You must also preserve the technique that allows you to move effectively as you get tired.
          - paragraph [ref=e346]: The first set creates leg fatigue through kicking and then asks you to integrate your kick into solid freestyle. Avoid allowing your legs to become completely inactive when you begin swimming. Maintain a kick appropriate for the pace and distance.
          - paragraph [ref=e347]: The middle set emphasizes breathing before progressively shorter freestyle swims. Use the stroke-and-roll repetitions to rehearse a low, controlled breath, then carry that movement into the longer repeat. The 300 should be swum at a faster pace than you did the 400, and your pace on the 200 faster still.
          - paragraph [ref=e348]: The final set alternates pulling and freestyle. During the pull repetitions, emphasize a firm catch and backward pressure. During the freestyle repetitions, preserve that pull while adding your kick. The odd-and-even pattern changes during the second group, requiring you to remain attentive late in the workout.
          - paragraph [ref=e349]: Choose one technical cue during each major set—your kick during the first set, your breath during the second, and your pull during the third. Focusing on every component of freestyle simultaneously becomes difficult when you’re tired.
          - paragraph [ref=e350]: If your technique deteriorates, take more rest or reduce the distance. For example, do three rounds instead of five during the kick set or three 100s instead of five in each final group.
          - paragraph [ref=e351]: The goal is to recognize when your mechanics begin to change and make useful adjustments before they break down completely.
          - heading "Frequently Asked Questions About Swimming Faster Freestyle" [level=2] [ref=e352]
          - heading "What freestyle skill causes the most problems?" [level=3] [ref=e353]
          - paragraph [ref=e354]: Breathing is a common source of freestyle problems because excessive or mistimed head movement can affect body position, rotation, the catch, and stroke rhythm. Lifting your head can cause your hips and legs to sink, and pulling your head too far to the side can move your body out of alignment.
          - paragraph [ref=e355]: Breathing isn’t always the only or primary problem, however. Body position, the pull, kick, timing, mobility, and conditioning can also limit performance. A qualified coach can help identify which component is having the greatest effect on your stroke.
          - heading "Why do my legs sink during freestyle?" [level=3] [ref=e356]
          - paragraph [ref=e357]: Your head position, breathing mechanics, core control, kick, speed, and body composition can all affect whether your legs sink while you’re swimming freestyle.
          - paragraph [ref=e358]: Pressing your head and upper chest slightly into the water, maintaining appropriate tension through your trunk and hips, and using a light, consistent kick can improve your alignment. Avoid forcing the chest downward or arching your back.
          - heading "Should I breathe to one side or both sides in freestyle?" [level=3] [ref=e359]
          - paragraph [ref=e360]: Use the breathing pattern that provides enough oxygen and allows you to swim effectively at your intended pace. During competition, many swimmers breathe primarily to one side because it supports their fastest rhythm or helps them see competitors, landmarks, waves, or the shoreline.
          - paragraph [ref=e361]: Practicing breathing to both sides can still be useful. You can breathe every three strokes or alternate your breathing side by length. You don’t need to breathe bilaterally (to both sides) during every swim, and you shouldn’t restrict your oxygen intake simply to maintain a fixed pattern.
